# Leonardo da Vinci

Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci  listen (Vinci, April 15, 1452–Amboise, May 2, 1519), better known as Leonardo da Vinci, was a Florentine polymath of the Italian Renaissance. He was a painter, anatomist, architect, paleontologist, botanist, writer, sculptor, philosopher, engineer, inventor, musician, poet, and urban planner. He died in the company of Francesco Melzi, to whom he bequeathed his projects, designs, and paintings. After spending his childhood in his hometown, Leonardo studied under the Florentine painter Andrea del Verrocchio. His first major works were created in Milan while in the service of Duke Ludovico Sforza. He then worked in Rome, Bologna, and Venice, and spent his final years in France at the invitation of King Francis I.
Often described as the archetype and symbol of the Renaissance man—a universal genius and humanist philosopher whose boundless curiosity is matched only by his inventive genius—Leonardo da Vinci is considered one of the greatest painters of all time and, probably, the most multi-talented person across multiple disciplines who has ever lived. As an engineer and inventor, Leonardo developed ideas far ahead of his time, such as the helicopter, the tank, the submarine, and the automobile. Very few of his projects were ever built (including the machine for measuring the elastic limit of a cable),​ since most were not feasible at that time.​ As a scientist, Leonardo da Vinci made substantial discoveries in the fields of anatomy, civil engineering, optics, and hydrodynamics, but since he never published his findings, they had little, if any, influence on subsequent science.​
His most famous historical association is with painting. Two of his best-known works, the Mona Lisa and The Last Supper, have been copied and parodied numerous times, as has his drawing of the Vitruvian Man. However, only about twenty of his works are known to exist, due mainly to his repeated (and sometimes disastrous) experiments with new techniques and his chronic inconsistency. This small number of creations, along with his sketchbooks containing drawings, scientific diagrams, and reflections on the nature of painting, constitute a legacy for successive generations of artists.

## Biography

### Childhood

He was born on Saturday, April 15, 1452, “at the third hour of the night,” that is, three hours after the Hail Mary: at 10:30 p.m.​ It has been debated whether his birth took place at the castle in Vinci—a town about 25 km as the crow flies from Florence—or at his mother’s home in Anchiano, a hamlet about two kilometers from Vinci.​ Leonardo, a descendant of a wealthy family of Italian nobles, was born out of wedlock: his father, Messer Piero Fruosino di Antonio (notary, chancellor, and ambassador of the Republic of Florence), impregnated Caterina, a young woman from a peasant family,​​ who has been suspected of having been a Circassian slave from the Middle East, from the Caucasus Mountains.​​ Martin Kemp, professor emeritus of art history at the University of Oxford, after researching the Tuscan archives, discovered documents that would confirm the date of his birth, which took place at his father’s country house, just over one kilometer from Vinci, and that his mother was a fifteen-year-old local peasant girl, Caterina di Meo Lippi. Piero was already betrothed, and the child was entrusted to his paternal grandfather, Antonio da Vinci, who likely gave the mother a certain sum as a dowry so she could marry. In tax records from 1457, Antonio declared that his grandson was living with him, and Caterina is listed as married to a peasant, Antonio di Piero Buti.
Leonardo—or Lionardo, according to his baptismal record—was baptized and spent his first five years at his father’s home in Vinci, where he was treated as a legitimate son. He had five godmothers and five godfathers, all of whom were villagers.​ He learned to read and write and acquired a knowledge of arithmetic; however, he learned virtually no Latin, the foundation of traditional education. The fact that his spelling was chaotic shows that his education was not without gaps; in any case, it was not that of a university student.​
At that time, modern conventions regarding personal names had not yet developed in Europe, so only prominent families used patronymic surnames. People in small towns were usually referred to by their first name, to which all sorts of details were added: their father’s name, their place of origin, a nickname, or the name of their master in the case of artisans, etc. Consequently, the artist’s name would be Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci, which means “Leonardo, son of Master Piero of Vinci”; the term “da” is not capitalized because it is not a surname.​ Leonardo himself simply signed his works as “Leonardo” or “Io, Leonardo” (“I, Leonardo”), so most experts attribute his works to “Leonardo” without the “da Vinci.” It is likely that he refrained from using his father’s surname because he was an illegitimate child. “Vinci” comes from “vinchi,” the name given to reed-like plants that grow along the Vincio stream and were used by Tuscan artisans.​
In 1457, when Leonardo was five years old, his mother married Antonio di Piero Buti del Vacca da Vinci, a local peasant, with whom she had five children.​ He was then taken in by his father’s family in the village of Vinci. Meanwhile, his father had married a sixteen-year-old girl from a wealthy Florentine family, Albiera degli Amadori.​ Since she had no children of her own, she showered Leonardo with affection, but she died very young due to complications during childbirth in 1464.​ Although he had been fully regarded as his father’s son from birth, Leonardo was never formally recognized as a legitimate son. His father married four times and had ten sons and two daughters by legitimate marriages.​ Of these relationships, young Leonardo had a very close bond with his father’s last wife, Lucrezia Guglielmo Cortigiani—an affection evident in a note in which he addresses her as “dear and sweet mother.”​
His paternal grandmother, Lucia di ser Piero di Zoso, a ceramist close to Leonardo, was likely the person who introduced him to the arts.​ A well-known omen recounts that a kite that had come down from the sky hovered over his cradle and touched his face with its tail.​​ Giorgio Vasari, a 16th-century biographer of Renaissance painters, recounts in *Le Vite* (1568), the story of a local peasant who asked ser Piero to have his son paint an image on a plaque. Leonardo then painted a depiction of a fire-breathing dragon, so skillfully executed that ser Piero sold it to a Florentine art dealer, who in turn resold it to the Duke of Milan. Having thus profited from the sale, Ser Piero bought a plaque decorated with a heart pierced by an arrow, which he gave to the peasant.​

### Training in Verrocchio’s Workshop

The young Leonardo was a nature lover who observed the world with great curiosity. He drew caricatures and practiced writing in mirror image in the Tuscan dialect. Vasari recounts an anecdote about the early stages of his career: “One day, Ser Piero took some of his drawings and showed them to his friend Andrea del Verrocchio, urging him to say whether Leonardo could devote himself to the art of drawing and whether he might achieve anything in this field. Andrea was greatly surprised by Leonardo’s extraordinary gifts and recommended to Ser Piero that he allow him to choose this craft,” so Ser Piero decided that Leonardo would begin working in Verrocchio’s workshop. Leonardo did not need to be asked twice, and, not content merely to practice this craft, he undertook everything related to the art of drawing.” Thus, beginning in 1469, Leonardo entered as an apprentice into one of the most prestigious art workshops under Andrea del Verrocchio, to whom he owes part of his excellent multidisciplinary training, during which he came into contact with other artists such as Sandro Botticelli, Perugino, and Domenico Ghirlandaio.​​ In fact, by the end of 1468, although Leonardo was registered as a resident of the town of Vinci, he traveled very often to Florence, where his father worked.​
Verrocchio was a renowned artist—and a highly eclectic one at that. He was trained as a goldsmith and blacksmith, but he was also a painter, sculptor, and metalcaster. He worked primarily for the powerful Lorenzo de’ Medici.​ Leonardo also worked with Antonio Pollaiuolo, whose workshop was located very close to Verrocchio’s.
After a year spent cleaning brushes and performing other minor tasks typical of an apprentice, Verrocchio introduced Leonardo to the many techniques practiced in a traditional workshop. Thus, in this context, Leonardo had the opportunity to learn the basics of chemistry, metallurgy, leatherworking, plasterwork, mechanics, and carpentry, as well as various artistic techniques such as drawing, painting, and sculpting in marble and bronze.​​ He also received training in color preparation, engraving, and fresco painting. Realizing his exceptional talent, Verrocchio decided to entrust his student with completing some of his own works.

His training in Verrocchio’s workshop went beyond the arts and extended to other cultural fields. He studied algorithmic calculation and demonstrated his deep knowledge by citing the two most prominent Florentine abacus masters, Paolo dal Pozzo Toscanelli and Leonardo Chernionese.​ Later, Leonardo mentioned Piero Borgi’s *Nobel opera de arithmética*, printed in Venice in 1484, which represents the knowledge of these schools of abacus masters.​
No works by Leonardo are known to exist from the period when he worked with Verrocchio. According to Vasari, he collaborated only on a painting titled The Baptism of Christ (1472–1475).​ Furthermore, according to legend, Verrocchio abandoned the completion of the work when he felt outshone by Leonardo’s skill, as Leonardo left his masterful touch on a small angel.​ Also, in accordance with the tradition that the apprentice was the one who had to pose,​ Leonardo is said to have served as the model for Verrocchio’s David, a bronze statue. Similarly, it is also believed that the portrait of Leonardo is the one depicting the archangel Raphael in the work *Tobias and the Angel* from Verrocchio’s workshop.​
In 1472, at the age of twenty, he was registered in the Red Book of the Guild of Saint Luke, the guild of artists and doctors of medicine, which in Florence was known as the “Campagnia de pittori.” One of his earliest known works dates from this period: *Landscape of the Arno Valley* or *Landscape of Santa Maria della Neve* (1473), a pen-and-ink drawing.​ This is how he began his career as a painter with works such as *The Annunciation* (1472–1475), and he refined the sfumato technique to a level of sophistication never before achieved.
In 1476, he is consistently mentioned as Verrocchio’s assistant; even after his father helped him establish his own workshop, he continued to collaborate with his master out of deep affection for him.​ During this period, he received private commissions and painted his first painting, *The Virgin of the Carnation* (1476). Court records from that same year indicate that he and three other men were accused in a case of sodomy—an illegal practice in Florence at the time— but all were acquitted.​ This document, which was based on an anonymous accusation, does not allow us to categorically state that Leonardo was homosexual.​
Leonardo soon distinguished himself as an engineer as well. In 1478, at the age of twenty-six, he volunteered to design the extension of the Baptistery of St. John. That year, he parted ways with his master after having surpassed him in every discipline. In this way, he became an independent master painter.

### In Milan and in the Service of Ludovico Sforza (1482–1500)

In 1481, the monastery of San Donato commissioned him to paint *The Adoration of the Magi*, but Leonardo never completed this painting, likely because he was disappointed or humiliated at not having been chosen by Pope Sixtus IV to decorate the Sistine Chapel in the Apostolic Palace of the Vatican in Rome, where there was fierce competition among several painters.​ Neoplatonism, which was in vogue in Florence at the time, may also have played an important role in his decision to move to a more open, academic, and pragmatic city like Milan under the Sforza family.​ This new environment was likely more in tune with his spirit, which was rooted in the empirical development of his many experiments.
Leonardo painted *The Virgin of the Rocks* (1483–1486) for the Confraternity of the Immaculate Conception, which was to be housed in the Church of San Francesco il Grande in Milan. This painting was the source of a conflict between the artist and the owners that lasted several years. Leonardo obtained the right to copy the work, but a legal dispute subsequently arose, which was not resolved until court rulings were issued and some friends intervened. Ultimately, two versions of the work were produced.

In Florence, Leonardo’s work did not go unnoticed. Lorenzo de’ Medici learned that he had created a silver lyre in the shape of a horse’s head. Impressed by the quality of his work, he sent Leonardo to Milan with it as a Florentine emissary, and also to work for the patron and Duke of Milan, Ludovico Sforza. The aim of this move was to maintain good relations with this important rival.​ He was likely accompanied by the musician Atalante Migliorotti.​ He also wrote a letter to the duke, which is found in the Codex Atlanticus, in which he describes Leonardo’s numerous and varied skills in the field of engineering and informs him that Leonardo is also a painter.​​ Consequently, Leonardo’s primary occupation from then on was that of an engineer. He was listed among the Sforza family’s engineers, and when he was sent to Pavia on June 21, 1493, he did so with the title of “ingéniarius ducalis.”​ Sforza commissioned him to carry out various projects and bestowed upon him the title “Florentine Apelles,” a distinction reserved solely for the greatest painters.​ In this capacity, he devoted himself to “organizing parties and spectacles with sumptuous decorations” at the palace and inventing stage mechanisms, such as those for the weddings of Ludovico Sforza and Beatrice d’Este and that of Anna Sforza and Alfonso I d’Este. He also painted several portraits of the Milanese court. Leonardo’s contact with Milan’s enlightened elites made him realize the gaps in his education.​
He worked on both the design for the dome of Milan Cathedral and the creation of the clay model for the mold of “Il Cavallo,” an equestrian statue in honor of Francesco I Sforza, Ludovico’s father. It was to be cast from seventy metric tons of bronze, a feat that represented a true technical achievement for the time.​ The statue remained unfinished for several years.​ When Leonardo completed the clay model for the mold and had already drawn up the plans for the casting process, the bronze was diverted to the manufacture of cannons, as the city had to defend itself against an invasion by Charles VIII of France.​
In 1490, he participated in a sort of conference of architects and engineers, who had gathered to discuss certain aspects of the completion of the dome of Milan Cathedral. That same year, he was called upon as a consultant, along with Francesco di Giorgio Martini, for the construction of Pavia Cathedral and was impressed by the equestrian statue of Regisole, of which he left a sketch. During this period, Leonardo reflected on technical and military projects. He improved clocks, looms, cranes, and many other tools. He also studied urban planning and proposed designs for ideal cities. He took an interest in hydraulic engineering, and a document from 1498 refers to him as an engineer in charge of work on rivers and canals.​ In 1495, a detailed list of funeral expenses suggests the death of his mother, Caterina.​​
Around 1490, he founded an academy bearing his name, where he taught for several years and recorded all his research in short treatises. He painted the fresco *The Last Supper* (1494–1498) for the Dominican convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie. In 1496, Luca Pacioli arrived in Milan and formed a close friendship with Leonardo, who created for him the plates that were engraved in his work *La Divina Proportione*.​ A little later, in 1498, he built the ceiling of the Sforzesco Castle.​
In 1499, the troops of Louis XII of France conquered the Duchy of Milan and deposed Ludovico Sforza, who fled to Germany with his nephew Maximilian I.​ On October 6, Louis XII entered the city and asserted his rights to the Visconti succession.​ One morning, Leonardo discovered that the French had destroyed his full-scale clay model of the horse for the equestrian statue of Francesco Sforza, because they had used it as a target for their archery practice.​ Louis XII considered cutting out the wall where *The Last Supper* was located to take it to France, just as Napoleon Bonaparte would consider doing centuries later.​ With the fall of the Sforza family, Leonardo entered the service of the Count of Ligny, Louis of Luxembourg, who asked him to prepare a report on the state of Tuscany’s military defenses. On December 4, Leonardo had 600 florins deposited at the Hospital of Santa Maria Nuova in Florence, which served as a bank. The unexpected return of Ludovico Sforza disrupted his plans, and he fled Milan with his assistant Salai and the mathematician Luca Pacioli.

### From Venice to Florence (1501–1508)

In March 1499, Leonardo worked as an architect and military engineer for the Venetians, who wanted to build defenses for their city.​​ For this reason, he devised systems to defend the city against a possible naval attack by the Turks, and among his proposals was the invention of a type of diving suit with a rudimentary helmet. The Turks did not attack, and the invention was never used. At the end of April, he returned to Florence, and there, after conducting a study of the waterways in Friuli, he proposed a system of locks to raise the level of the Isonzo River and thus flood an entire region surrounding Venice.​

He returned to Venice in April 1500 to stay there for a couple of months after having been in Mantua in the company of the mathematician friar Luca Pacioli. In Mantua, he painted a portrait of Isabella d’Este. A letter dated April 4, 1501, in which Pierre de Nuvola responds to the Duchess of Mantua, regarding Leonardo, states that “his mathematical studies have taken him away from painting”; Leonardo continued to carry out extensive research. In 1501, at the convent of the Santissima Annunziata, he received approval to make a sketch of what would become *The Virgin and Child with Saint Anne and Saint John the Baptist*, a work that aroused such admiration that “men and women, young and old” flocked to see it “as if they were taking part in a great festival.”​​ He spent a brief period in Rome at Villa Adriana in Tivoli. He was working on *The Virgin of the Spindles*, a commission from Florimond Robertet, the secretary of state to Louis XII of France.
In 1502, he was approached by Prince Cesare Borgia, Duke of Valentinois and son of Pope Alexander VI, and was appointed “condottiero captain and general engineer”. He remained in the Marches and in Emilia-Romagna to inspect the fortresses and recently conquered territories, filling his notebooks with numerous observations, letters, working sketches, and copies of works he consulted in the libraries of the cities he visited.​ There he met again with Niccolò Machiavelli, a “spy” from Florence in the service [citation needed] of the Borgias.
On October 18, 1503, he returned to Florence, where he worked as an architect and hydraulic engineer.​ He re-enrolled in the Guild of Saint Luke and devoted two years (1503–1505) to the preparation and execution of *The Battle of Anghiari*, a mural measuring seven by seventeen meters,​​ while Michelangelo painted *The Battle of Cascina* on the opposite wall.​ Both works have been lost. Michelangelo’s work is known from a 1542 copy by Aristotole da Sangallo,​ and Leonardo’s painting from preparatory sketches and various copies of the central section, of which Rubens’s is undoubtedly the most notable.​ It appears that the causes of the work’s deterioration were either the fire used to dry the paint more quickly or the quality of the materials used; it was later covered, probably by a fresco by Giorgio Vasari.​
Leonardo was often consulted as an expert—for example, to study the stability of the bell tower of San Miniato al Monte and when choosing the site for Michelangelo’s David—on which his opinion differed from Michelangelo’s. It was during this period that he presented to the city of Florence his plan to divert the Arno River, intended, on the one hand, to create a navigable waterway capable of connecting Florence to the sea and, on the other, to help control the terrible floods. This period was important for Leonardo’s scientific education.

### From Milan to Rome

In 1504, he returned to work in Milan, which at that time was under the control of Maximilian Sforza thanks to the support of Swiss mercenaries. Many students and those interested in painting met or worked with Leonardo in Milan, including Bernardino Luini, Giovanni Antonio Boltraffio, and Marco d’Oggiono. His father died on July 9, and Leonardo was excluded from the inheritance due to his illegitimate birth; however, his uncle would later name him his sole heir.​ That same year, Leonardo conducted anatomical studies and attempted to organize his countless notes. It was also during this period that he began work on *La Gioconda* (1503–1506, and later 1510–1515), a work generally considered a portrait of Lisa Gherardini, or, by her married name, Monna Lisa del Giocondo. However, there are numerous interpretations of this painting that remain a subject of debate and discussion.
In 1505, he devoted himself to studying the flight of birds, which led him to write the *Codex on the Flight of Birds*. From then on, observations, experiments, and reconstructions followed one another at a rapid pace.​ A year later, the Florentine government allowed him to meet with the French governor of Milan, Charles d’Amboise, who kept him with him despite protests from Florence. Leonardo found himself caught in the middle of disputes between the French and the Tuscans, and was pressured by the court to finish *The Virgin of the Rocks* with his student Ambrogio de Predis, while he was working on *The Battle of Anghiari*.​
In 1507, Leonardo became the sole heir of his uncle Francesco, but Leonardo’s brothers initiated proceedings to have the will amended.​ Leonardo appealed to Charles d’Amboise and Florimond Robertet to intervene on his behalf;​ Louis XII returned to Milan, and Leonardo once again became the organizer of the festivities held in the Lombard capital.
In 1508, he lived with the sculptor Giovanni Francesco Rústica at the home of Piero di Braccio Martelli in Florence,​ but later left and moved to Milan, to the Porta Orientale, in the parish of Santa Babila.Louis XII soon returned to Italy and entered Milan in May 1509. He soon led his armies against Venice, and Leonardo accompanied the king as a military engineer and took part in the Battle of Agnadel.​ In 1511, Governor Charles d’Amboise died, and in 1512, following the Battle of Ravenna, France withdrew from Italian territory. This second period in Milan allowed Leonardo to delve deeper into his research in pure science. In 1509, the publication of Giorgio Valla’s *De expendentis te fugiendis rebus* had a profound influence on him.​
In September 1513, Leonardo went to Rome, where he worked for Pope Leo X, a member of the wealthy and powerful Medici family. At that time, Raphael and Michelangelo were very busy working in the Vatican.​ Given Sangallo’s success, Leonardo received only modest commissions and it appears he participated neither in the construction of the numerous, relatively unimportant Roman fortresses nor in the beautification of the capital. His painting also failed to gain recognition, so he turned to another specialty—perhaps his favorite—hydraulics, and he undertook a project to drain the Pontine Marshes, which belonged to Duke Giuliano II de’ Medici. In 1514, he created the series *The Floods*, which was a partial response to the great work Michelangelo had executed on the vault of the Sistine Chapel.
“The Medici created me, the Medici destroyed me,” Leonardo wrote, no doubt to underscore the disappointments of his stay in Rome. He believed he would never be allowed to demonstrate his abilities by completing a major commission. His instability, his tendency to become easily discouraged, and his difficulty in finishing what he had started were likely also well known.​

### Final Years in France

In September 1515, the new king of France, Francis I, recaptured Milan after winning the Battle of Marignano. In November of that same year, Leonardo designed a new plan for the layout of the Medici quarter in Florence. On December 19, he was present at the meeting between Francis I and Pope Leo X held in Bologna.​​​ Francis I commissioned Leonardo to create a mechanical lion that could walk, with the detail that its chest would open to reveal the fleur-de-lis.It is not known for what occasion this lion was conceived, but it may have been related to the king’s arrival in Lyon or to the peace talks between the king and the pope.
In 1516, he left for France along with his assistant, the painter Francesco Melzi, and Salai, who accompanied them as far as Milan.​​ His new patron and protector, King Francis I of France, settled them in the house where he had lived as a child, the Château du Clos-Lucé, near the Château d’Amboise. He was the “king’s chief painter, chief engineer, and chief architect,” and received a pension of 10,000 escudos. At the French court, they were more interested in him as a painter than as an engineer, although in Italy it was the latter role that was most highly valued.​ The fact that Francis I gave him the Château de Clos-Lucé can be understood as a message to Leonardo to “do whatever he wanted.” He was not the first artist to receive this honor, as Andrea Solario and Giovanni Giocondo had preceded him a few years earlier.​ Leonardo designed the royal palace at Romorantin, which Francis I intended to build for his mother, Louise of Savoy: it was to be a small town, for which he planned to divert a river to supply it with water and fertilize the surrounding countryside. In 1518, he took part in the celebrations for the baptism of the Dauphin and the wedding of Lorenzo de’ Medici to a niece of the French king. That same year, Salai left Leonardo once again and returned to Milan, where he died in a duel on January 19, 1524.
On April 23, 1519, Leonardo, who had been ill for several months, drew up his will before a notary in Amboise. He asked for a priest to hear his confession and administer last rites.​ He died on May 2, 1519, in Cloux, at the age of 67. In accordance with his last will, sixty beggars followed his funeral procession, and he was buried in the Saint-Hubert Chapel on the grounds of the Château d’Amboise. Leonardo never married and had no children; he bequeathed his entire body of work to be published by his favorite disciple, Francesco Melzi, who had been his student since the age of ten. He gave him, above all, his manuscripts, notebooks, documents, and instruments. Francesco, after having accompanied him during his stay in France, remained with Leonardo until his death and administered his estate for the following fifty years. However, he did not publish any of Leonardo’s works nor sell any of the numerous paintings, such as the Mona Lisa, which was still in his workshop. Leonardo’s vineyards were divided between Gian Giacomo Caprotti da Oreno—another student and disciple whom Leonardo held in high regard and who had entered his service at the age of fifteen—and his servant Battista di Vilussis. The land was bequeathed to Leonardo’s brothers, and his servant received a black coat.​

## Relationships and Influences

### Leonardo in Florence: Teachers and Contemporaries

In 1466, when Leonardo began his apprenticeship with Andrea del Verrocchio, the sculptor Donatello—who had been Verrocchio’s own teacher—died. The painter Paolo Uccello—who had experimented with perspective and influenced the development of landscape painting—was already very old, and the painters Piero della Francesca and Fra Filippo Lippi, the sculptor Luca della Robbia, and the architect and writer Leon Battista Alberti were around 60 years old. The most famous artists of the next generation were Andrea del Verrocchio, Antonio Pollaiuolo, and the sculptor Mino da Fiesole.
Leonardo spent his youth in a house in Florence whose walls were adorned with works by these artists and by Donatello’s contemporaries, such as Masaccio—whose figurative and realistic frescoes are imbued with emotion— and Lorenzo Ghiberti, whose Gates of Paradise display great compositional complexity, combining his work as an architect with meticulous attention to detail. Piero della Francesca had conducted a detailed study of perspective and was the first painter to carry out a scientific study of light. His works, as well as the treatises of Leone Battista Alberti, must have had a profound effect on the young artists, and in particular on the observant Leonardo and his works.​​​
Masaccio’s depiction of the nude in *The Expulsion of Adam and Eve from Paradise*, featuring an Adam whose genitals were not covered by a fig leaf, creates a highly expressive image of the human form that would have a major influence on painting, especially because the figures are depicted in three dimensions thanks to an innovative use of light and shadow, which Leonardo developed in his own works. Renaissance humanism and its influence, as seen in Donatello’s *David*, can be appreciated in Leonardo’s later paintings, particularly in his *St. John the Baptist*.​
At that time, Florence was ruled by Lorenzo de’ Medici and his younger brother Giuliano (1453–1478), who was killed during the Pazzi conspiracy. Ludovico Sforza, who ruled Milan from 1479 to 1499 and to whose court Leonardo was sent as an ambassador for the Medici, was also his contemporary.​​ It was also through the Medici that Leonardo came into contact with the older humanist philosophers such as Marsilio Ficino, a Neoplatonist, and Cristóforo Landino, author of commentaries on classical works. Giovanni Pico della Mirandola was likewise associated with the Medici court. Leonardo later wrote a marginal note in his diary: “The Medici created me, the Medici destroyed me”; the meaning of this comment is still a matter of controversy.
Although the three giants of the Renaissance—Leonardo, Michelangelo, and Raphael—are often mentioned together, they were not of the same generation. Leonardo was 23 when Michelangelo was born and 31 when Raphael was born. Raphael died in 1520, one year after Leonardo. By the time Michelangelo died, Leonardo had already been dead for 45 years.​​

### Assistants and Students

Gian Giacomo Caprotti da Oreno,​ known as “il Salaino” (“the little devil”) or Salai, was described by Vasari as “a charming and handsome young man with fine, curly hair, who enchanted Leonardo.”​ Salai entered Leonardo’s service in 1490 at the age of 10. Their relationship was not an easy one. A year later, Leonardo made a list of the boy’s misdeeds, calling him a “thief,” “liar,” “stubborn,” and “glutton.” The “little devil” had stolen money and valuables on at least five occasions and had squandered a fortune on clothes and twenty-five pairs of shoes. However, Leonardo’s notes from the early years of his relationship with the boy contain numerous images of the teenager. Salai served as his aide, servant, and assistant for the next thirty years.
In 1506, Leonardo took on 15-year-old Francesco Melzi, the son of a Lombard aristocrat, as a student. Melzi became Leonardo’s lifelong companion and is considered his favorite student. He remained in France with Leonardo and Salai, and stayed with the master until his death. Salai left France in 1518 and returned to Milan, where he built a house on the vineyard of one of Leonardo’s estates, which the painter eventually bequeathed to him. In 1525, Salai died a violent death, perhaps murdered or as a result of a duel.​
Salai produced a number of paintings under the name “Andrea Salai,” but, although Vasari claimed that Leonardo “taught him many things about painting,”​ his work is considered less valuable than that of other students of Leonardo, such as Marco d’Oggiono or Giovanni Antonio Boltraffio. In 1515, he painted a nude version of the Mona Lisa, called “Monna Vanna.”​ Upon his death in 1525, the Mona Lisa owned by Salai was appraised at 105 lire, an exceptionally high value for a small-scale portrait.​
In addition to Giovanni Antonio Boltraffio and Marco d’Oggiono, other students were part of Leonardo’s workshop when he returned to Milan, including Ambrogio de Predis, Bernardino dei Conti, Francesco Napoletano, and Andrea Solario—the latter two being less well-known.

### Personal Life

Leonardo da Vinci had many friends who were renowned in their respective fields or who exerted significant influence during his time. For example, the mathematician Luca Pacioli, with whom he collaborated on a book; Cesare Borgia, in whose service he spent two years; Lorenzo de’ Medici; and the physician Marcantonio della Torre. He met Niccolò Machiavelli, with whom he had a close friendship, and his rival Michelangelo. His friends also included Franchino Gaffurio and Isabella d’Este. Leonardo does not appear to have had close relationships with any women, except for Isabella, whom he painted during a trip that took him to Mantua—a portrait that seems to have served as the basis for a painting that is now lost.​ He was also friends with the architect Jacopo Andrea da Ferrara until the latter’s assassination.​
Beyond these friendships, Leonardo kept his private life a secret. His life, his extraordinary inventive abilities, his “exceptional physical beauty,” his “infinite grace,” his “great strength and generosity,” and the “formidable breadth of his spirit”—as described by Vasari—have aroused curiosity.​ Numerous authors have speculated on various aspects of Leonardo’s personality. His sexuality has been the subject of studies and analysis. This trend began in the mid-16th century and intensified during the 19th and 20th centuries, with Sigmund Freud standing out among scholars.​
Leonardo’s closest relationships were with his students Salai and Francesco Melzi. Melzi wrote that Leonardo’s feelings were a mixture of love and passion. Since the 16th century, these relationships have been described as erotic. Since then, much has been written about his alleged homosexuality and its role in his art, particularly the androgynous impression evident in his *Bacchus* and, more specifically, in some of his drawings.​
Leonardo was a passionate admirer of nature and animals, to the point of becoming a vegetarian and buying caged birds only to set them free. He was also an excellent musician. He was left-handed, which would explain his use of mirror writing.

## Work

### Painting

Despite the growing recognition and admiration Leonardo has garnered in recent years as a scientist and inventor, his fame has rested on his creations as a painter—various works, whether authenticated or attributed to him, that are considered great masterpieces of the world’s cultural heritage.​​
His paintings are renowned. On the one hand, they have been copied and imitated by students; on the other, they have been the focus of debate and controversy among specialists. Among his qualities, it is worth highlighting the innovative painting techniques he employed, his sense of composition and subtle use of color shading, his profound knowledge of human and animal anatomy, botany, and geology, his use of light, his interest in physiognomy, and his ability to capture the way humans express emotions and use facial gestures. Above all, he mastered the technique of sfumato and the interplay of light and shadow. All these qualities come together in works such as the Mona Lisa, The Last Supper, and The Virgin of the Rocks.​

#### Early Works

Leonardo’s earliest known work is a section of *The Baptism of Christ* by Verrocchio and his students. Another painting that appears to date from this period is *The Annunciation*. One of them is small, measuring 59 centimeters long and 14 centimeters high. It is a predella intended to be placed at the base of a larger composition—in this case, a painting by Lorenzo di Credi from which it has been separated. The other is a much larger work, measuring 217 centimeters long.​ In these two Annunciations, Leonardo has depicted the Virgin Mary seated or kneeling on the right side of the image, and an angel in profile approaching her from the left. Much of the work is focused on the movement of the angel’s clothing and wings. Although this work was previously attributed to Domenico Ghirlandaio, it is now almost universally attributed to Leonardo.​ Between 1478 and 1482, he painted the Benois Madonna, a work attributed to him but whose date of creation is highly debated. Dated to around 1474–1476, another painting attributed to the Florentine artist is a small portrait, Ginevra de’ Benci. The Virgin of the Carnation, presumed to have been painted between 1478 and 1480, is another of his works from this period.

#### The 1480s

In the 1480s, Leonardo received two major commissions and began work on another piece that was equally significant in terms of composition. Two of these were never completed, and the third was the subject of negotiations regarding its completion and payment. One of these paintings was *Saint Jerome*. Liana Bortolon associates this painting with a difficult period in Leonardo’s life; signs of melancholy can be read in his diary: “I thought I was learning to live, but I was only learning to die.”​ The bold and innovative composition of *Saint Jerome*, with its elements of landscape and personal drama, also appears in another great unfinished work, the *Adoration of the Magi*, commissioned by the friars of San Donato in Scopeto. It was a highly complex painting, and Leonardo produced numerous drawings and preparatory studies, including a very detailed one for the linear perspective of a classical architectural ruin that serves as the backdrop to the scene. In 1480, the Florentine painter created *The Lady with an Ermine*, but in 1482, at the request of Lorenzo de’ Medici, he left for Milan to win the favor of Ludovico Sforza, and thus abandoned that painting.​​ The third major work from this period was *The Virgin of the Rocks* (of which there were two versions, both attributed to Leonardo),​ which was commissioned by the Confraternity of the Immaculate Conception in Milan. The painting was intended to fill a large altarpiece that had already been constructed, and its execution was nearly as complex as that of *The Adoration of the Magi*, despite featuring only four figures; it depicts a landscape rather than an architectural background. The painting was completed, and two versions were produced: the one that remains in the confraternity’s chapel, and a second version created several years later, with the addition of the halos and John the Baptist’s staff.

#### 1490s

Between 1495 and 1498, Leonardo painted *The Last Supper*, which depicts the final meal shared by Jesus with his disciples; it was painted directly onto a wall of the convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan. He worked from dawn to dusk without stopping to eat, only to then go without food for the next three or four days—a practice that met with the incomprehension of the convent’s prior.It is a masterpiece in its conception and characterization, which has earned the admiration of artists such as Rubens and Rembrandt. The work has had to be constantly restored due to the technique used by Leonardo; after some time, it was described by a witness as a work that was “completely damaged.”​ It is one of the most reproduced works of art.​ Between 1499 and 1500, Leonardo drew the work *Saint Anne, the Virgin, the Child, and Saint John the Baptist*, also known as the *Burlington House Carton*.

#### 1500s

Between 1503 and 1506, he worked on a small portrait, *La Gioconda*, likely the most famous painting in Western art. The work depicts Lisa Gherardini, wife of Francesco del Giocondo. It was a painting very dear to Leonardo, and he kept it with him until his death. Its current appearance is deceptive due to a thick layer of darkened varnishes, the removal of which is being delayed because of the risks involved. Despite this, the painting is in an unusually good state of preservation, as it shows no visible signs of repairs, overpainting, or retouching—a very rare occurrence for a painting from this period.
Another notable work is *The Virgin, the Infant Jesus, and Saint Anne*, painted around 1510, which has been copied many times. The work influenced Michelangelo, Raphael, and Andrea del Sarto in particular, and through them, Pontormo and Antonio da Correggio. The compositional style was adopted in particular by Venetian painters such as Tintoretto and Paolo Veronese. One of Leonardo’s last paintings was that of Saint John the Baptist, for which he used Salai as a model.​

#### Drawings

Leonardo was not a prolific painter, but he was, in contrast, a very productive draftsman; he filled his journals with small sketches and highly detailed drawings to record everything that had caught his attention. In addition to his notes, there are numerous studies for his paintings, some of which are preparatory sketches for works such as The Adoration of the Magi, The Virgin of the Rocks, and The Last Supper.​ His first dated drawing was Landscape of the Arno Valley from 1473, in which the river, the mountains, Montelupo Castle, and, in the distance, the farmlands are rendered in great detail.​​
Among his most famous drawings is the Vitruvian Man, a study of the proportions of the human body, the Head of an Angel, *The Virgin of the Rocks*, and *The Virgin, the Infant Jesus with Saint Anne and Saint John the Baptist*, which was a large drawing (160 x 100 cm) in black and white chalk on colored paper; in it, he used the sfumato technique. This theme of Saint Anne, along with that of the Holy Family, would predominate in Leonardo’s work from 1500 to 1517. It does not appear that Leonardo produced any paintings based on this drawing, but a painting quite similar to it is *The Virgin, the Infant Jesus, and Saint Anne*.
Among other drawings of interest are numerous “caricature-like” studies which, although exaggerated, appear to be based on the observation of live models. Vasari notes that when Leonardo saw a person with an interesting face, he would follow them throughout the day so he could observe and sketch them in detail.​ There are also numerous studies of exceptionally beautiful young men with unusual facial features, characteristic of the so-called “Greek profile”—a profile typical of many Greek statues—which have been associated with Salai. Leonardo had painted Salai wearing various outfits and costumes; it should be noted that Leonardo was famous for creating decorations and costumes for traditional processions. Other drawings, often meticulous, depict studies of clothing. Another example of Leonardo’s curiosity is a drawing of Bernardo di Bandino, one of the assassins of Giuliano de’ Medici in the Pazzi conspiracy, after he was hung from one of the windows of the Palazzo del Capitano di Giustizia in Florence on December 29, 1479.​

### Sculpture

None of his sculptures have survived. The sculptural project by Leonardo about which the most is known is that of an equestrian statue intended to depict Francesco Sforza, father of Ludovico il Moro. It would have surpassed in size the other two bronze equestrian statues of the Renaissance: Donatello’s Gattamelata in Padua and Verrocchio’s Monument to Bartolomeo Colleoni in Venice. Leonardo created a clay model, known as the “Great Horse.” It was intended to be an eight-meter-tall bronze statue to be erected in Milan. Seventy metric tons of metal were prepared for casting. The monument remained unfinished for several years, which was not unusual for Leonardo. In 1495, the bronze was used to cast cannons for the duke in an attempt to save Milan from the French under the reign of Charles VIII of France in 1495. Through a private initiative, a statue based on his plans was built in New York in 1999 and donated to the city of Milan, where it was erected.

## Leonardo as an Observer, Scientist, and Inventor

### Notebooks

Renaissance humanism does not view science and the arts as mutually exclusive. Leonardo’s studies in science and engineering are as impressive and innovative as his artistic work, and were recorded in his diaries and notebooks, which comprise 13,000 pages of text and drawings, linking art and natural philosophy (the precursor to modern science). These notes were made and updated daily throughout Leonardo’s life and travels. He constantly strove to make observations of the world around him, aware and proud of being, as he defined himself, an “illiterate,” self-taught, and keen observer of natural phenomena that often lay far removed from what was taught in school.
These journals are mostly written in mirror writing, also known as “reversed script,” which may have been due more to practical reasons—since this method of writing was faster—than to encryption, as has so often been suggested. Since Leonardo wrote with his left hand, he found it more comfortable to write from right to left.
His notes and drawings—the earliest of which date back to 1475—reveal a wide variety of interests and concerns, including simple shopping lists or records of money borrowed. There are compositions for paintings, studies of details and tapestries, studies of facial expressions, animals, dissections, babies, studies in botany and geology, war machines, flying machines, and architectural works.
These sketchbooks (originally loose sheets of various sizes and types, given away by his friends after his death) have found their way into major collections such as those on display at Windsor Castle, the Louvre, the National Library of Spain, the Ambrosian Library in Milan, and the Victoria and Albert Museum and the British Library in London. The British Library has made a selection available online (BL Arundel MS 263). The Codex Leicester, or Codex Hammer, is the only major work of this kind in private hands.
Leonardo’s notebooks appear to have been intended for publication, given that many of the pages have a format and order that facilitate editing. In many cases, a single subject—for example, the heart or the human fetus—is treated in detail, both in the text and in the drawings, on a single page.​ This method of organization also minimizes the loss of those pages that were later distributed. The reason they were not published during Leonardo’s lifetime remains unknown,​ although it is thought that it may have been because the society of his time—and especially the Catholic Church—was not ready to accept anatomical studies.

### Scientific Studies

Leonardo’s scientific method was based primarily on observation: “Science was the captain; practice was the soldier.” His scientific research focused exclusively on topics that were accompanied by practical application.Leonardo sought to understand phenomena by describing and illustrating them in great detail, without placing too much emphasis on theoretical explanations. His studies on the flight of birds and the movement of water are undoubtedly remarkable. Because he lacked a basic education in Latin and mathematics, contemporary scholars overlooked the scholar Leonardo; however, his remarkable self-education led him to teach himself Latin.
In the 1490s, he studied mathematics under Luca Pacioli and produced a series of skeletal drawings of regular solids to be included in the book *Divina Proportione* (1509). He was fascinated by the idea of the absolute and the universal.​ However, his mathematical background was fundamentally practical, with the limitations typical of the abacus users of his time, and included some knowledge of Euclidean geometry, perspective, and mechanics, in line with what theorists of his era knew. Nevertheless, Leonardo conceived an instrument with an articulated system designed to provide a mechanical solution to Alhazen’s problem—an essentially technical issue that attests to a deep understanding of the properties of conic sections.​
His knowledge of physics was quite limited; he was never an artilleryman and never formulated any theory regarding ballistics. Nevertheless, as some of his diagrams attest, Leonardo was able to intuit—based on observations of a water jet—that there was no rectilinear portion in the trajectory of an artillery projectile, contrary to what was believed at the time. But he did not delve further into a line of inquiry that Tartaglia and Benedetti had developed and that Galileo brought to fruition. Similarly, he studied topics related to light and optics; and in hydrology, the only law he ever formulated concerned watercourses. As for the field of chemistry, he perfected a still and conducted some research on alchemy while in Rome.​ While, in architecture, Alberti and Francesco di Giorgio were concerned with the strength of beams, they did not approach the problem through mathematical formulations as Leonardo did; he was interested in the problem of bending and succeeded in defining “laws”—albeit imperfect ones— for example, regarding the elastic line in the case of beams with different cross-sections. Thus, he eliminated the elastic modulus and the moment of force to which Jordanus Nemorarius had alluded.

### Anatomy and Medicine

Leonardo’s early training in human anatomy began while he was studying under Andrea del Verrocchio, who insisted that all his students have a knowledge of anatomy. As an artist, he soon became a master of topographic anatomy, producing numerous studies of muscles, tendons, and other visible anatomical features.
He dissected the bodies of criminals as part of his anatomical studies. Working conditions were particularly grueling due to problems with hygiene and the preservation of the bodies. As a renowned artist, he received authorization to dissect human cadavers at the Santa Maria Nuova Hospital in Florence and, later, at hospitals in Milan and Rome. From 1510 to 1511, he collaborated with the physician Marcantonio della Torre, and together they compiled a collection of anatomical research featuring more than two hundred drawings by Leonardo; these were published under the misleading title *Treatise on Painting* in 1680.
He produced many drawings of human anatomy—including bones, muscles, and tendons; the heart and vascular system; the reproductive system; and other internal organs—as well as diagrams illustrating the functioning of the eye. These observations sometimes contain inaccuracies due to the limitations of knowledge at the time—for example, a study he conducted on blood flow. He produced one of the earliest drawings of a fetus inside the uterus, and the first known scientific observation of arterial stiffness in response to a heart attack. As an artist, Leonardo closely observed the effects of age and human emotion on physiology, studying in particular the effects of rabies. He also drew numerous models, some of whom had significant facial deformities or visible signs of illness. He also studied the anatomy of numerous animals. He dissected cows, birds, monkeys, bears, and frogs, with the sole purpose of comparing the anatomical structure of these animals with that of humans. He also conducted studies on horses.

### Engineering and Inventions

Leonardo da Vinci was part of the technical movement of the Renaissance and, as such, had both immediate and more distant predecessors. Among these were Konrad Kyeser, Taccola, Roberto Valturio, Filippo Brunelleschi, Jacomo Fontana, and Leon Battista Alberti, to whom he likely owed a great deal.​
Some of them were more influential figures, with an even more well-rounded spirit and an even broader curiosity than Leonardo’s. This was the case, for example, with Francesco di Giorgio Martini, who was Leonardo’s superior during the construction of the Milan Duomo and from whom Leonardo learned a great deal.​ Probably because he was less busy than Martini due to a smaller backlog of commissions, Leonardo was both more meticulous and, above all, capable of changing his approach.
Along with Heron of Alexandria, Leonardo is considered an important precursor of a number of modern machines. However, beyond the wonder inspired by the author’s forward-thinking imagination, it quickly becomes clear that the actual functioning of the machines was likely not his primary concern. Just like the monk Eilmer of Malmesbury, who in the 11th century forgot to attach a tail to his flying machine, Leonardo’s inventions run into numerous difficulties: the helicopter would have spun around like a top, the diver would have suffocated, and the paddleboat would never have been able to move forward [...]​ However, it has also been suggested that Leonardo intentionally introduced specific errors into his designs to prevent others from successfully building his devices.​
In a letter addressed to Ludovico Sforza, he claimed to be capable of building all kinds of machines, both for the defense of cities and for their siege. After fleeing to Venice in 1499, he found employment as an engineer and developed a system of mobile barriers to protect the city from land attacks. He also planned the diversion of the Arno River to irrigate the Tuscan fields, facilitated transportation, and hindered maritime access to Pisa, Florence’s rival.​

His notebooks contain a large number of “inventions” that are both practical and realistic, notably hydraulic pumps, crank mechanisms such as the machine for machining screws, fins for mortar shells, a steam cannon,​ the submarine, various automatons, the war chariot, the automobile, floats for “walking on water,” solar energy concentration, the calculator, the diving suit, the double hull for ships, and ball bearings. As for the bicycle, its invention is not clearly attributed to him.​
A careful examination of the sketches indicates, however, that several of these technologies were either borrowed from immediate predecessors—Francesco di Giorgi Martini’s hydraulic turbine, Taccola’s articulated drive chain— or were part of an older tradition—the hydraulic hammer was known in the 13th century; siphons and aqueducts were already evident in the work of the Roman Frontinus; and recreational automata had already been described in the Hellenistic period […]—.
But Leonardo was also an innovator. He was probably one of the first engineers of his time to take an interest in the mechanical working of metals, particularly gold, the most malleable of them all. His originality is evident in the flying machine and in a few textile machines, in which he had the opportunity to apply his keen sense of observation to the regularity of movements. The mechanical loom, the carding machine, and the “sheet-shaving” machine likely make him the first to attempt to mechanize industrial manufacturing. He conceived the machine for polishing mirrors—which involved solving a number of problems to achieve smooth, flat, or concave surfaces—during his stay in Rome while studying the production of images. Paradoxically, Leonardo da Vinci took little interest in inventions of his time that are now considered very important, such as the printing press, although he did create one of the earliest graphic depictions of a printing press.​
If war can be said to respond to any need, it is the “pazzia bestialissima”—“savage madness.” Leonardo studied weapons while maintaining a distance from their use.

In 1502, Leonardo designed a 240-meter bridge for a civil engineering project commissioned by the Ottoman Sultan Beyazid II. The bridge was intended to span the estuary known as the Golden Horn. Beyazid abandoned the project because he considered the construction impossible. Leonardo’s vision was revived in 2001 when a small bridge based on his concept was built in Norway. On May 17, 2006, the Turkish government decided to build Leonardo’s bridge over the Golden Horn.
For most of his life, Leonardo was fascinated by flight. He produced numerous studies on the flight of birds as well as plans for various flying machines, such as a primitive helicopter called an “aerial screw,” a parachute, and a bamboo hang glider.​ Although most of these designs are considered unfeasible, the delta wing has been built and, after adding stabilizers, has flown successfully. It is possible, however, that Leonardo believed flight systems similar to those of bats held the greatest potential. He also invented the aerodynamic wind tunnel for his experiments.
The Clos Lucé Museum in Amboise (France), the Il Castello Museum at the Castle of the Counts of Guidi in Vinci (Italy), and the Leonardo da Vinci Museum of Science and Technology in Milan contain numerous full-scale models based on the instructions in his sketches and notebooks, as well as explanations of Leonardo’s work.
Leonardo also studied architecture. He was influenced by the work of Filippo Brunelleschi and planned to raise the height of the Baptistery of St. John in Florence as well as to create a lantern tower for the Milan Cathedral.​ He often used the octagonal shape for religious buildings and the circle for military structures.​ In the wake of the plague epidemic that struck Milan between 1484 and 1485, he designed a theoretical ideal city with optimized traffic flow and high-quality living conditions, in a vision defined not by social distinctions but by functional ones, modeled after the organs of the human body. He also worked on gardens. Unfortunately, many of his architectural studies have been lost.
