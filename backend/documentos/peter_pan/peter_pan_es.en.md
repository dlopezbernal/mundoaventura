# Peter Pan

Peter Pan is a fictional character created by Scottish writer James Matthew Barrie for a play that premiered in London on December 27, 1904, titled *Peter Pan and Wendy*.
Peter Pan is a boy who never grows up; he is ten years old and hates the adult world. He is always accompanied by his fairy, Tinker Bell. The dust she sprinkles gives Peter the ability to fly indefinitely. He lives in Neverland, an island populated by pirates, Native Americans, fairies, and mermaids, where he has numerous adventures alongside his friends, the Lost Boys.

## Inspiration for the Character

The character of Peter Pan was inspired by the Llewelyn Davies siblings. The Llewelyn Davies family was friends with James Barrie, who developed the idea for Peter Pan and Neverland based on his time spent with and friendship with the Davies’ children. He often staged small plays with the Davies children and actively participated in their games.

## The Character in the Play

Barrie posits that, before they are born, all babies are birds; this is the origin of Peter Pan, a boy who flew out the window of his bedroom while his parents slept, because he had overheard them talking about the things he would have to do when he grew up. Believing himself to be a bird, he flew straight to Kensington Gardens; there, the fairy Tinker Bell found him and took him to Neverland, where he met Indians, mermaids, fairies, and other fantastical creatures that inhabited that place.
Peter Pan is a preteen between the ages of 11 and 16—slender, red-haired, and brown-eyed—who wears a suit made from plant materials and items found in the Neverland jungle. He also carries a dagger, which he often uses as a weapon, and a pan flute. In the novel, Peter has an immature, self-centered, selfish, and—at times—cruel personality because he lives doing whatever he pleases without any real responsibility; that is why he has a terrible fear of growing up and living in a world full of rules and limits.
His main enemy is James Hook, captain of the Jolly Roger crew, whose right hand Peter cut off in a sword duel; ever since then, he has been pursued by Hook’s crew, who seek to capture and kill him; however, this doesn’t seem to worry him much, and he spends his time mocking and taunting Hook and all his pirates.
Peter flies to the Darlings’ window almost every night because he enjoys the stories Wendy tells her brothers, and one night, after retrieving his shadow, Peter decides to take them to Neverland so that Wendy can become the mother of the Lost Boys.
When Wendy decides to return home with her brothers and the Lost Boys, Peter promises Wendy he’ll come back to hear her stories.
At the end of the play, he is described as the boy who guides the souls of children who have died before their time to the Afterlife.

## Abilities

Peter Pan’s archetypal ability is his endless youth. In *Peter Pan and Wendy*, it is explained that Peter must never remember his own adventures or what he learns about the world in order to remain a child. The unauthorized prequel by Barry and Pearson attributes Peter’s eternal youth to his exposure to stardust, a magical substance that has fallen to Earth.
Peter’s ability to fly is explained, albeit inconsistently. In *The Little White Bird*, Peter is able to fly because, like all babies, he is half-bird. In both the play and the novel, he teaches the Darling children to fly using a combination of “precious, wonderful thoughts” (which became “happy thoughts” in the Disney film) and fairy dust; it is unclear whether “wonderful thoughts” alone are sufficient (the novel suggests this was merely a silly diversion from fairy dust being the true source), or whether fairy dust was required. However, in Barrie’s dedication to the work *Peter Pan, the Boy Who Wouldn’t Grow Up*, the author attributes the idea of fairy dust as necessary for flying to more practical considerations.
In *Hook*, the adult Peter is unable to fly until he recalls his “happy memories.” The ability to fly is also attributed to stardust—apparently the same as fairy dust—in the *Starcatchers* prequels. Peter has an effect on all of Neverland and its inhabitants when he is there. Barrie says that although Neverland appears different to every child, the island “comes alive” when Peter returns from his trip to London. In the chapter “The Mermaid Lagoon” in the book Peter and Wendy, Barrie writes that there is hardly anything Peter is not capable of doing. He is a skilled swordsman, rivaling even Captain Hook, whose hand he cut off in a duel. He has remarkably keen eyesight and hearing. He is an expert at mimicry, imitating Hook’s voice and the Crocodile’s ticking. In the 2003 film, the mermaids speak using dolphin sounds, which Peter can understand and speak.
In *Peter and Wendy* and *Peter Pan in Scarlet*, there are several references to Peter’s ability to imagine things into existence, such as food, although this ability plays a greater role in *Peter Pan in Scarlet*. He also creates imaginary windows and doors as a sort of physical metaphor for ignoring or avoiding his companions. He is said to be able to sense danger when it is near.
In *Peter and Wendy*, Barrie states that the legend of Peter Pan—which Mrs. Darling heard as a child—was that when children die, he accompanies them on part of their journey to their final resting place so they won’t be afraid; in this way, he resembles the Greek god Hermes in his role as a psychopomp.
In the original play, Peter says that no one must ever touch him (though he doesn’t know why), and the stage directions specify that no one should do so during the play. Wendy approaches Peter to give him a “thimble” (kiss), but Tinker Bell stops her.

## Related Characters

### Tinker Bell

She was the fairy who adopted and raised Peter Pan, and is possibly the reason he is somewhat conceited. She is very jealous, vain, and mischievous—so much so that she even encourages the Lost Boys to attack Wendy, telling them she is a bird that Peter wants them to eliminate. She isn’t actually evil, but fairies are so small that they only have room for a single emotion.
To show her affection for Peter, she rushes to drink his poisoned medicine, nearly killing herself, but he manages to save her.

### Wendy Darling

She is a beautiful twelve-year-old girl who dreams of a wolf cub on a strange island that seems to be a mix of forest and jungle. Peter Pan appears in her dreams, but it isn’t until several days later that she actually meets him.
She accompanies Peter to Neverland because she likes the idea of being a mother and wakes up her two younger brothers so they can go with her, too. In the book, she acts like a mother and even says things her own mother used to say, such as: “My goodness, sometimes I think single women are to be envied” and “Sometimes children are more of a curse than a blessing.”
Captain Hook points out to her that she ran away from home and that she may have been a disappointment to her parents. Both Peter and Hook love her as a mother, for her talent for storytelling and her great affection and sensitivity. She later returns home with her brothers and the Lost Boys.
It has been said that Barrie invented the name “Wendy” for this story, but in reality, it was already in use in the United States and England, although it was extremely rare. The Peter Pan stories popularized the name. Wendy is the English diminutive of Gwendolin. Other variants include: Guendolen, Guendolina, Guendalina, Gundelina, Gundelinda, Guvendolina, Gwyneth, Gwen, and Wendi.

### Captain Hook

Captain James Hook is a fictional character from *Peter Pan*, created by J. M. Barrie as a pirate captain and the flying boy’s nemesis. Captain of the Jolly Roger, he wears an iron hook where his right hand (left in some adaptations) should be, which was cut off by Peter Pan and eaten by a crocodile. He seeks to kill Peter Pan to avenge the loss of his hand while being pursued by the crocodile, which has become obsessed with the taste of his flesh.

### John and Michael Darling

They are Wendy’s younger brothers, aged nine and four, respectively. They love hearing the stories about Peter Pan that their older sister tells them at night.
They get along quite well with the Lost Boys, as well as with Tigerlily, the Indian princess. Although they are characters in the book, they play a minor role in the story.

### The Lost Boys

They are a gang of boys who live with Peter Pan and Tinker Bell in the underground house so that Hook won’t find them. They are: Slightly, Tootles, Nibs, Curly, and the Twins.

### The Pirates

The main antagonists of the story are a gang of cruel and bloodthirsty bandits who crew the Jolly Roger. They came to Neverland to explore and steal all the treasures and jewels they could find. They are led by James Hook, a famous and feared pirate whose right hand was cut off by Peter Pan and fed to a giant crocodile—which liked the captain’s taste so much that it follows him everywhere, hoping to devour him. Ever since then, Hook has used a hook as his right hand, which he also uses as a weapon when fighting. The crew accuses their captain of having forgotten the traditional activities of pirates—such as stealing and plundering—since they spend their days searching for Peter Pan’s hideout to kill him.
Hook’s first mate is the clumsy Mr. Smee.

### The Picaninny

They are a tribe of the same ethnicity as Native Americans. They are Peter Pan’s close friends and the pirates’ sworn enemies. Tigridia is the princess of the village.

### George and Mary Darling

They are the parents of Wendy, John, and Michael, and are portrayed as serious and responsible adults. George works at a bank and strikes Wendy as somewhat strange or even frightening. What their children don’t know is that George was once friends with Peter Pan and was one of the Lost Boys.
This explains George’s comment when he sees the Jolly Roger flying alongside his wife and children: “I once saw that very ship when I was a boy…” As for Mary, the book mentions that she has a “Special Kiss” on her cheek meant for someone special—the same one that Wendy has, and, in turn, her daughter Jane. All three special kisses are meant for Peter Pan.

## Appearances

On December 27, 1904, the character made his theatrical debut in a play titled *Peter Pan, the Boy Who Wouldn’t Grow Up* or *Peter Pan and Wendy*, which premiered in London and was later adapted into a children’s book in 1911. Meanwhile, the episode featuring Peter Pan from the book *The Little White Bird* was adapted by Barrie for publication in 1906 as part of a children’s book titled *Peter Pan in Kensington Gardens*, which was illustrated by Arthur Rackham.

## In Popular Culture

### Movies

Peter Pan first appeared on screen in the 1924 American silent adventure film *Peter Pan*, released by Paramount Pictures as an adaptation of the original play.
Since its 1953 animated film, The Walt Disney Company has continued to use Peter Pan as a character. The studio featured him in the sequel *Return to Neverland* and in its theme parks as a meet-and-greet character and as the star of the *Peter Pan’s Flight* ride, a dark ride suspended on elevated tracks. He also appears as a supporting character in the television series *House of Mouse* and *Jake and the Never Land Pirates*, as well as in various Disney video games, such as the *Kingdom Hearts* series. In the movie *Chip 'n Dale: Rescue Rangers*, Sweet Pete—the “actor” who played the character in the 1953 film—serves as the main antagonist.
In 1991, *Hook* was released, the first film featuring the character as an adult, played by Robin Williams.
*Peter Pan: The Great Adventure* (2003) starred Jeremy Sumpter as Peter.
In 2015, the film *Pan* was released, starring Levi Miller as Peter, an orphan who traveled to Neverland.
The character appeared in the 2023 film *Peter Pan & Wendy*, played by Alexander Molony; the story is a live-action adaptation of the 1953 animated film.

### Television

The character stars in the 1990 animated series *Peter Pan and the Pirates*.
Peter Pan has appeared in the series *Once Upon a Time*, played by Robbie Kay; he wants the heart of a believer to stay alive, and together with the Lost Boys and his shadow, he kidnaps other children to search for Henry. He is a recurring villain in the series, and was originally a man named Malcolm, the father of Rumpelstiltskin.

### Music

In 1980, the Neapolitan singer-songwriter Edoardo Bennato released the album *Sono solo canzonette* (They’re Just Little Songs), inspired by and dedicated to Peter Pan, the other characters, and their world, and in protest against record labels and the business behind the music industry.
In 2002, the Madrid-based singer-songwriter Ismael Serrano released his album titled *La traición de Wendy* (*Wendy’s Betrayal*), in which he alluded to Barrie’s novel, particularly its ending and the promise Peter Pan and Wendy make to each other at the end of the book.
In 2010, the song “Hey Peter Pan” by Spanish singer-songwriter José Riaza appeared on his live album *El folk es el hogar*. Previously, he had released three songs with references to the character with his rock band Tragicomi-K: “Ella y yo” (2002), “Ella y yo II/La traición de Wendy” (2006), and “Heredando revoluciones” (2008). The second of these songs would appear on José Riaza’s album *Cualquier tiempo pasado* (2014) as “La traición de Wendy.”

## Themes

The most widely explored theme in the story appears to be the fear of growing up and facing the challenges of adulthood, represented by the figure of Peter Pan and the Lost Boys, although the latter are eventually adopted by the Darlings and grow into serious, responsible adults.
Some commentators also see sexual content in the story: Wendy’s sexual awakening and Peter’s Freudian feelings toward the mother figure, along with the conflicts and conflicting emotions between Wendy and Tinker Bell, each representing a different stereotype of womanhood throughout the story. The relationship between Peter Pan and Wendy has also been interpreted from a psychoanalytic perspective.​

## Public Sculptures

The original statue in Kensington Gardens by sculptor George Frampton was commissioned by Barrie and erected overnight on April 30, 1912, as a May Day surprise for the children of London. There are seven statues cast from the original mold. The other six are located at:
Egmont Park, Brussels, Belgium, 1924
Bowring Park, St. John’s, Newfoundland, Canada, 1925
Johnson Park, Camden, New Jersey, United States, 1926
Queens Gardens, Perth, Western Australia, 1927
Sefton Park, Liverpool, England, 1928
Glenn Gould Park, Toronto, Ontario, Canada, 1929
A statue by Paul Montfort was commissioned by the City of Melbourne, Australia, in 1926 and is now located at the Melbourne Zoo.
A statue by Alex Proudfoot RSA, director of the Glasgow School of Art, was erected at the Mearnskirk Children’s Hospital in Glasgow in 1949, commissioned by Alfred Ellsworth in memory of his friend Dr. John A. Wilson, the first superintendent of Mearnskirk Children’s Hospital, who had been a friend of J.M. Barrie’s school.​
A statue by Ivan Mitford Barberton was commissioned by Vyvyan and Gwen Watson in memory of their son Peter and unveiled in 1959 at the Red Cross Hospital, a memorial to the fallen in the Western Cape, South Africa.​
A pair of statues by Cecil Thomas—one depicting Peter Pan and Tinker Bell, and the other Wendy and the Darling children—have been on display at the Dunedin Botanic Garden in Dunedin, New Zealand, since the 1960s.​
Two bronze casts of Alistair Smart’s statue, originally commissioned by the Angus Milling Company in 1972, are located in Kirriemuir, Scotland—one in the main square and the other in the Peter Pan Garden, in Barrie’s birthplace, now owned by the National Trust of Scotland.​
A bronze statue by Diarmuid Byron O’Connor was commissioned by Great Ormond Street Hospital in London and unveiled in 2000, depicting Peter blowing fairy dust, with Tinker Bell added in 2005.​
